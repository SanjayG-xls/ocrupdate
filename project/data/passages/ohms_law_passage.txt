An electric current flows in a circuit when a cell pushes charges through the wires. The push that drives the current is the potential difference, which is measured in volts. Every conductor opposes the flow of current to some extent, and this opposition is called its resistance. Resistance is calculated by dividing the potential difference across a conductor by the current flowing through it. The basic unit of resistance is the ohm (Ω), while current is measured in amperes (A). For example, a bulb that has a potential difference of 12 volts across it and a current of 2 amperes through it has a resistance of 6 ohms. If the current stays the same, a conductor with a greater voltage must have a higher resistance. If two conductors have the same voltage, the one with the smaller current has a higher resistance. An ammeter measures the current in a circuit, while a voltmeter measures the potential difference across a component. Knowing resistance helps us choose the right wires and bulbs for a circuit.
